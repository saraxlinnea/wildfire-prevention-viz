An Ounce of Prevention

National wildfire activity, 1983-2026

As of June 18, 2026, more than 2.6 million acres have burned nationally, about 63% above the 10-year average to date. Peak fire season starts in summer. In 2025 the Forest Service treated 35% fewer acres for wildfire risk than the year before.

Wildfires need three things: something to burn (grass, brush, or forest), weather dry enough to carry fire, and a spark (lightning, power lines, campfires, and other human sources). This page tracks national acres burned since 1983, federal prevention work reported by agencies, and western dryness each fire season. Those stories use different geographies and calendars, so they live on separate charts. The point is to show what rose or fell together in the record, not to claim that any single line caused a fire season.

This is the bottom line most people want first: how many acres burned nationally each year, and whether 2026 is running high for this point in the season. Most acres burn in western states, especially California, Oregon, and the northern Rockies, but this line counts the entire country. Figures through June 18, 2026 are year-to-date only, not a full calendar year.

Prevention work and weather drivers live here. Treatment charts count acres federal crews treated (thinning, prescribed fire, clearing brush). Dryness charts describe how stressful the western fire season was for fuels and atmosphere. Neither tab alone explains every acre burned nationally, because sparks, wind, and firefighting also matter.

Terms used on this tab

Fire managers combine weather readings, fuel conditions, and maps to plan each season. Below are the measures this page charts, in everyday language.

ERC · Energy Release Component
A fuel-focused fire danger index. It estimates how intensely fuels could burn if a fire starts, based on how dry live and dead vegetation has become. It does not include wind or slope, but it is one of the main numbers dispatchers watch when deciding preparedness levels. Higher ERC in May through September usually means a busier western fire season.
VPD · Vapor pressure deficit
A measure of how strongly the air pulls moisture from plants and soils. Think of it as atmospheric thirst. When VPD is high, grasses and fine fuels dry out quickly even if a drought map still looks moderate. VPD is physical weather, not a fire model, but it often rises ahead of large fire years in the West.
DSCI · Drought Severity Coverage Index
How much land sits in drought categories on the U.S. Drought Monitor. It is useful for water supply, agriculture, and long-term vegetation stress. On this page it tracks annual acres burned less closely than ERC or VPD, because a map can show drought while a wet week temporarily lowers fire danger.
Treatment acres
Acres where federal agencies report hazardous-fuels work. That can mean thinning dense forest, burning piles under controlled conditions, or clearing brush near roads and communities. The count is workload, not a report card. Work next to homes and evacuation routes matters more for safety than remote acreage far from people.

Federal fuels treatment

Federal prevention work
Federal hazardous fuels treatment (2003-2025)

What you are looking at: combined acres the U.S. Forest Service and Interior Department (BLM, Park Service, Fish and Wildlife, Bureau of Indian Affairs) say they treated for hazardous fuels each year. The long history comes from the joint federal HFR report for fiscal years 2003 through 2021. Recent years use agency totals verified for this page. Treatment includes prescribed fire, mechanical thinning, and pile burning. The line moves with congressional funding, hiring, smoke windows, and which forests were prioritized. A higher number does not automatically mean communities are safer, because location and maintenance matter more than the national total.

Same years, two measures
Treatment and acres burned on one timeline

What you are looking at: two different national measures on the same timeline. The green line is federal treatment acres (left axis). The orange line is national acres burned (right axis). They answer different questions. Treatment is planned work reported by agencies. Acres burned is the outcome of weather, fuels, ignitions, and suppression. When both are high in the same year, that often means a severe fire year that also demanded emergency spending, not proof that prevention failed. When treatment is steady in quieter years, agencies are using the window before the next hot summer.

Researchers ask which dryness measures track wildfire acres best in the West. Published work ranks short-term fire danger (ERC) and atmospheric dryness (VPD) above broad drought indices. This tab shows that ranking, then tests it against this page’s data from 2010 through 2025. It also shows where fires burned (western vs Alaska vs South). These are statistical patterns only. They do not explain every fire or prove cause and effect. Exploratory · not causal

Proxy ranking (literature vs this page)

Scientists compare annual measures like ERC to annual acres burned and report correlation coefficients. A higher number means the two series moved together more closely in that study’s window. It does not mean one caused the other. Lightning, human ignitions, forest management, and firefighting all shape outcomes. The table below summarizes what western fire-climate studies typically find, then shows this page’s exploratory check in the last column.

# Proxy What studies find Citation This page (r)
1 ERC (fire danger) Among the strongest short-term predictors of large-fire area in the West; often beats longer drought indices Riley et al. 2013; Williams et al. 2015 0.82
2 VPD (atmosphere) Warm-season / fire-season VPD tracks western forest burned area; fuel-aridity pathway under climate change Abatzoglou & Williams 2016; Williams et al. 2015 0.81
3 Drought (DSCI / PDSI-like) Useful context; longer-timescale drought indices usually weaker than ERC for annual large-fire area Riley et al. 2013 (PDSI vs ERC) 0.10

The ordering matches the literature: ERC and VPD sit at the top; broad drought indices sit at the bottom for annual western acres. Exact numbers differ because studies use other regions, forest-only fires, or longer records.

In plain termsERC and VPD measure similar dryness signals and often rise together (correlation about 0.94 here). ERC is more directly tied to fire danger ratings firefighters already use. DSCI is still worth watching for water and vegetation stress, but it is a weaker match to annual acres burned on this page.

Western acres burned (geography for coupling)

Most U.S. wildfire acres burn in the West, but not all. This chart compares western GACC totals (solid orange) to the national NIFC total (dashed red) on the same scale. GACC regions are geographic coordination areas used in federal fire reporting. Aligning western dryness measures with western acres (as in the scatter above) matches geography better than pairing western weather with national totals.

How to read this
Outcomes chart national acres burned (NIFC, 1983 onward). That is the acreage that actually burned, regardless of cause. Year-to-date and forecast numbers are incomplete or speculative. The shaded band on the fire chart uses prior full calendar years. Header callouts use NIFC’s same-date ten-year average, which is a different comparison.

Drivers & context explain federal treatment work and western fire-season dryness (ERC, VPD, DSCI). Treatment is reported effort. Dryness indices describe conditions that make fires easier to spread once started. They use different geographies and calendars than the national burn total.

Coupling asks which dryness measures track western acres best in this dataset (2010 through 2025). ERC and VPD align more closely than drought maps alone. Regional share charts show whether the West, Alaska, or the South carried each year’s total. Supplementary correlation tables are collapsed below the main charts. None of this proves that one factor caused another.

What this page is not. A fire forecast, a smoke model, or proof that a budget cut caused a fire season. It is a structured look at public data so you can see what moved together and what did not.
Scientific limits
Geography. National acres include every state. ERC, VPD, and western DSCI describe western fire-prone areas. Pairing western dryness with western acres is more consistent than pairing western weather with a national burn total that Alaska or the South may dominate.

Calendar. Burn acres and DSCI use calendar years. Interior treatment uses federal fiscal years starting October 1. Fire season runs from spring through fall, but these charts annualize months into one number.

Correlation. Correlation describes how two lines move together in a short sample. It does not control for El Niño, vegetation recovery, housing growth in fire-prone areas, or how hard crews fought each fire.

Policy. Treatment acres measure work reported, not lives or homes saved. Thinning and prescribed fire can lower intensity near communities when maintained, but extreme weather can overwhelm fuels work.

Forecasts. The AccuWeather 2026 range is a published projection, not observed fire data.
Policy context
In 2025 the Forest Service treated 2.6 million acres for wildfire risk, down from 4.1 million in 2024. In March 2026 USDA announced plans to close 57 of 77 Forest Service research stations; many study fire behavior and smoke forecasting. Same date in 2022 saw about 3.1 million acres burned YTD, the highest in NIFC's table; 2026 ranks second.
On the data and sources
  • Acres burned: total U.S. figures, all land types, calendar year, NIFC annual statistics. Standardized reporting from 1983; pre-1983 figures are not comparable.
  • 2026 point: partial year through June 18 only (2,627,549 acres). Not a full-year figure.
  • 10-year baseline band: min-to-max of the ten prior calendar years of NIFC acres burned. Computed from chart data, not the CRS flat 7.2M reference.
  • Forest Service treatment: NPR analysis of USFS FACTS database, cross-checked by Center for Western Priorities. Consistent figures from 2023 onward only.
  • Interior Department treatment: BLM, NPS, BIA, and FWS lands, fiscal year starting October 1, doi.gov, 2018-2024.
  • DSCI: annual average of weekly readings, U.S. Drought Monitor API. National (conus) and NWS Western Region (aoi=WR). Available from 2000. 2026 values are 24-week averages through June 16.
  • VPD: western U.S. fire-season mean from gridMET via OPeNDAP (Abatzoglou 2013). May through September, west of 100°W, 1979-2025.
  • ERC: western fire-season mean Energy Release Component from gridMET (NFDRS fuel model G). Same geography and season as VPD. scripts/extend_erc.py; annual averages.
  • Western acres burned: sum of NICC GACC acres for NW, NR, GB, RM, SW, NO, SO (EB+WB before 2015 Great Basin merge), 2010-2025. Built by scripts/build_western_acres.py from NICC annual reports. Excludes Alaska, Eastern, and Southern GACCs.
  • 2026 forecast: AccuWeather published 2026 wildfire season projection, 5.5 to 8 million acres.
  • Research stations: USDA Forest Service reorganization announced March 31, 2026.
  • Correlations: Coupling tab ranks ERC / VPD / drought proxies with literature citations, then shows this page’s exploratory matrix (correlation-matrix.csv, notebook). Literature and page r values use different methods; not causal evidence.
  • Data processing: drought from U.S. Drought Monitor API; western fire-season VPD from gridMET (Abatzoglou 2013). CSVs and notebooks in the GitHub repository.

This page is not claiming that cutting prevention in 2025 caused the 2026 fire season. It shows two things happening at the same time and leaves the conclusion to the reader.
